Student Loans vs Other Debt: Which to Pay Off First

By Robert — Debt Free Empire·October 22, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Rank every debt by interest rate, not balance, and attack anything above 8% before touching student loans.
  • Pay off credit cards before student loans because 20% APRs cost roughly four times as much interest as a 5% loan.
  • Put unpaid tax debt first in most cases, because after required notices the IRS can levy wages and bank accounts without a court order.
  • Pay student loans slowly if you qualify for income-driven forgiveness or lack a one-month emergency fund.
  • The avalanche method saves more money on mixed debt than the snowball method because it targets the highest interest rate.

Whether to pay off student loans first or other debt comes down to interest rate, not balance: attack anything above roughly 8% (usually credit cards) before touching student loans, then put extra payments on the highest-rate debt while paying minimums on the rest. This guide covers where federal loan protections change the answer, how tax debt fits, and when paying student loans slowly is the smarter move.

Should you pay student loans first? The rate-first answer

The fastest way to pay off student debt is to check your loan's interest rate before throwing extra money at it. If your student loans sit at 4-6% while a credit card charges 24%, every dollar sent to the card saves you far more interest, so the card goes first. Student loans also carry protections most debts don't: income-driven repayment and forgiveness programs can lower your required payment if money is tight, which frees up cash for higher-rate debts.

Whether to pay off student debt fast has no single answer until you list every balance and rate side by side. Our free debt calculator shows your debt-free date for each order you might choose, so you can see the difference before committing.

Should you pay off student loans fast or slow — when does speed actually cost you?

The rate-first answer: pay student loans fast only when their rate beats every other debt you owe and your emergency fund is set. Otherwise, minimums plus investing the difference usually wins.

When fast payoff wins

Aggressive payoff makes sense at high rates, especially private loans above roughly 7%, or when the monthly payment itself is crushing your paycheck-to-paycheck cash flow. It also wins psychologically: if the debt-free date on your dashboard is what keeps you going, that motivation has real value. A zero-based budget, where every dollar has a job, shows you exactly how much extra you can safely throw at it (debtfreeempire.com/resources/how-to-budget-for-beginners).

When slow payoff wins

Slow wins when your rate is low, your federal loans carry protections like income-driven plans, or you carry credit card balances at much higher rates. Every extra dollar on a 5% loan is a dollar not killing a 24% card. Speed costs money whenever a cheaper debt waits.

If you have student loans and credit card debt, which should you pay off first?

Pay the credit cards first, almost every time. Credit card APRs commonly run 20% or higher, while federal student loan rates are set by Congress and are typically far lower (Federal Student Aid). Every dollar on a 24% card costs you roughly four times what it does on a 6% loan.

The rate decides the order, not the balance. A $5k card balance outranks an $18k student loan at 5%.

The rate-vs-balance decision matrix for mixed debt

Priority order, step by step

When you hold student loans, credit cards, and tax debt at once, you rank them by rate and consequence, not by balance or by which one annoys you most. Tax debt usually goes first, because after required notices the IRS can levy wages and bank accounts without a court order (IRS Publication 594). Credit cards go second at whatever APR you carry, often 20% or higher. Student loans go last if the rate is under roughly 7%, because extra payments there earn the smallest return.

Here is the matrix in one pass: list every debt with its rate, its minimum payment, and what happens if you miss. Attack the top of that list with every spare dollar while paying minimums on everything below it. A $5k credit card at 24% APR costs about $1,200 a year in interest; an $18k student loan at 5% costs $900. Same math, different damage.

The card wins, and the balance size is irrelevant. If the student loan rate beats your card rate instead, flip the order and let the numbers, not your guilt, decide. Once your order is set, log each payment and watch your debt-free date move; if you want a second pair of eyes on your plan, our support page walks through it with you at debtfreeempire.com/support.

How fast can you pay off $5k in debt on a typical budget?

$5k is the payoff most people finish first, so the timeline depends almost entirely on how much extra you can throw at it each month. At $100 extra per month on a card with a typical rate, you're looking at roughly three years. At $250 extra, you cut that to about 17 months. At $500 extra, you're debt-free in under a year. These are illustrative figures for a $5,000 balance at 22% APR with a $100 minimum (so $100 extra means $200 a month in total), not promises, since your rate and minimums set the baseline.

The practical move is to pick the extra amount you can repeat every payday, put it on your highest-rate balance, and watch the debt-free date move as you log each payment. A zero-based budget where every dollar has a job makes that extra amount visible instead of guessed. Our free budget and debt app shows that date and lets you enter everything manually, which is exactly what one reader ended up preferring after auto-tracking failed with their bank (Robert Daniels covers the payoff math in more detail). If $100 is what survives a paycheck-to-paycheck month, start there and raise it after the first payoff win.

What's the fastest realistic plan to pay off $18k or $20k in debt?

Worked example: $18k

Say you owe $18k total: $5k in student loans at 5% ($55 minimum), $10k in credit cards at 22% ($250 minimum), and $3k on a car loan at 7% ($95 minimum). With $600 a month beyond the $400 in minimums, you pay $1,000 a month in total, and each freed-up payment rolls forward. Avalanche puts the extra on the cards first: the cards are gone in month 14, the car in month 16, and the student loans in month 20, with about $1,940 in total interest. Snowball (car, then student loans, then cards) takes 22 months and costs about $3,120 in interest, roughly $1,180 more, because the 22% cards survive longest. Interest is added monthly at one-twelfth of the APR, with no new charges.

Worked example: $20k

Add tax debt and the order changes. Say you owe $12k in credit cards at 22% ($300 minimum), $5k in student loans at 5% ($55 minimum), and $3k to the IRS (assume an 8% effective rate and a $100 minimum). With $600 extra, you pay $1,055 a month in total. Clearing the IRS balance first finishes it in month 5, the cards in month 18 and the student loans in month 22, with about $2,970 in interest. Putting the cards first would save roughly $430 in interest, so paying the IRS first is the price of removing collection risk, not the cheapest order on paper. If you cannot pay a tax balance outright, an installment agreement spreads it over time; the IRS explains the options on its payment plans and installment agreements page. Then avalanche the cards, then the loans.

How does tax debt change your payoff priority order?

Tax debt usually jumps the queue. After the IRS sends its required notices, including a final notice and a 30-day window to request a hearing, it can levy wages and bank accounts without a court order (IRS Publication 594). That makes $5k owed to the IRS more urgent than $18k of student loans at a fixed rate. One caution: federal student loans in default can also be collected without a court order, through wage garnishment and Treasury offset of tax refunds, after notice (Federal Student Aid). Credit card companies and private lenders generally have to sue and win a judgment first.

The practical order: minimum payments on everything, then attack tax debt first, then high-rate credit cards, then student loans. Call the IRS about an installment agreement before you pay anything extra elsewhere, because a payment plan you can afford beats a levy you cannot.

One nuance: if your tax balance is on an approved installment agreement and you are current on it, the IRS generally cannot levy while the agreement is in effect, with exceptions (IRS Topic 202). Rate-based ordering applies again, and credit cards move ahead of it.

What's the difference between avalanche and snowball when you have mixed debt types?

Avalanche puts every spare dollar on the highest interest rate first, regardless of balance. Snowball puts it on the smallest balance first, regardless of rate. With mixed debt, the math usually favors avalanche: credit card APRs average well above 20%, while federal student loans often sit in the 4-7% range, per Federal Reserve G.19 data on card rates and Federal Student Aid on loan rates. That gap means $20k of card debt costs more each month than $18k of student loans at similar balances.

Snowball still has a use case: if your smallest debt is a $5k card, killing it fast frees up cash flow and gives you a visible win. The honest caveat for our own Debt Free Empire app: it shows both payoff orders side by side, but it does not auto-import transactions, so you enter them manually.

When does paying student loans fast actually hurt you?

Forgiveness and income-driven plans

If you are on an income-driven repayment plan and expect any balance to be forgiven, extra payments can reduce the amount that would have been forgiven, so they may cost you more than they save. A low-rate federal loan with a realistic forgiveness path can be a better place to hold debt than a 24% credit card. Plan rules and eligibility have changed in recent years, so check your plan's current terms on studentaid.gov before accelerating anything.

The emergency fund exception

A $500 surprise on a credit card at 24% costs less than a $500 surprise with no cushion, because the cushion keeps new debt from forming. If you have less than one month of expenses saved, pause extra student loan payments and build that first. Then run your rate-vs-balance order and put every spare dollar where the interest rate says it belongs.

FAQ

Is it better to pay off student loans fast or slow?

Fast payoff makes sense for private loans above 7% or when payments strain your cash flow. Slow wins when your rate is low, your federal loans carry income-driven repayment or forgiveness protections, or you owe higher-rate credit card debt that every spare dollar should attack first.

Should I pay off my credit card or student loan first?

Pay the credit card first, almost every time. Card APRs commonly run 20% or higher while federal student loan rates are typically far lower, so each dollar on the card saves roughly four times the interest. Keep paying student loan minimums to stay current, and rank by rate, not balance.

How can I pay off $20,000 in debt in a year?

Paying off $20,000 in a year takes about $1,800 a month in total payments at a blended 15% interest rate (about $1,670 with no interest), which is unrealistic for most budgets. If unpaid tax debt is in the mix, ask the IRS about an installment agreement first, since after required notices it can levy wages and bank accounts without a court order. Otherwise, attack the highest-rate balance and expect a two to three year timeline.

Does paying off student loans early hurt your credit score?

Paying a loan off early can cause a small, temporary dip when the account closes and your mix of installment debt changes, but the effect is minor and fades. The bigger trade-off is financial: extra dollars on a low-rate loan with a forgiveness path may vanish, while dollars on a 24% card save real interest.

What's the snowball method and does it work for student loans?

Snowball means putting every spare dollar on the smallest balance first, regardless of rate, to free up cash flow and score a visible win. It works for student loans when one small loan is blocking your motivation or monthly budget. The math usually favors avalanche, though, since card APRs far exceed typical student loan rates.
